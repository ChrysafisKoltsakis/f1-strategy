## 1. Where the project sits

The project asks how to decide, live, whether a car should pit now or stay out, using only public timing data. Each part of the system corresponds to an established line of work:

| System part | Research area | Closest prior work |
|---|---|---|
| Tyre / pace model (`03`) | Tyre degradation and lap-time modelling | Cappello 2025, Todd 2025 |
| Hindsight-optimal baseline (`04`) | Dynamic programming for pit strategy | Carrasco Heine 2022, Aguad 2024 |
| Trigger classifier (`05`) | Learning pit decisions from team behaviour (imitation learning) | Heilmeier 2020 (VSE), Sasikumar 2025, Deep-Racing 2023 |
| Live pit-now-vs-wait (`09`/`10`) | Stochastic DP with random safety cars | Carrasco Heine 2022 (SDP), Heilmeier 2020 (Monte Carlo) |

| Streaming layer (`08`/`09`) | Stream processing | Kreps 2011, Armbrust 2018 |

## 2. Related work

### 2.1 Optimising pit strategy

- **Dynamic programming.** [Carrasco Heine et al. (2022)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5f3f09703c505e6ab40c2ca0216df81f/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`carrascoheine2022`] formulate pit strategy as a DP over laps, compound and tyre wear, minimising total race time. This is the formulation `04` follows. They extend it to a **stochastic DP** with random yellow flags and rain, and find the stochastic solution **delays pit stops to benefit from a possible yellow flag**, beating the deterministic DP most where yellow flags are frequent. That is the same effect our live comparison now models with a historical per-lap safety-car rate.
- **Game theory.** [Aguad et al. (2024)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/79d6f7e3b10b5b3a8d18fa6208575976/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`aguad2024`] model two drivers as a zero-sum Stackelberg game solved with DP, including yellow flags. A driver who races strategically against one who ignores competition raises their winning odds by more than 15%. This is the strongest evidence that our single-car model leaves value on the table (section 4d).
- **Other optimisers.** Mixed-integer nonlinear programming jointly with energy management ([Fieni et al. 2025](https://consensus.app/papers/details/af557156731e53159c1e4832e0541b99/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`fieni2025`]); a trackside simulation-and-optimisation framework ([Avella et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/93a74b7340ec540c978dfbaf66e7110b/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`avella2026`]); genetic algorithms on FastF1 data ([Silva et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d23ca6406f3d5314bbc3f310f9cf2a9c/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`silva2026`]).

### 2.2 Race simulation and random events

[Heilmeier et al. (2018)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/45334fb7ed24553d91ceef70eb6205ec/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`heilmeier2018`] built a lap-wise race simulation from public timing data, covering tyre wear, fuel, pit stops and overtaking. [Heilmeier et al. (2020b)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/680c1336abd35de984f3e20adc6c500e/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`heilmeier2020mc`] add Monte Carlo modelling of accidents, safety cars, starts and lap-time variability to assess how robust a strategy is. Our historical per-lap safety-car rate is a lighter version of the same idea: a probability per lap instead of a full simulation.

### 2.3 Learning pit decisions from data

- **Virtual Strategy Engineer.** [Heilmeier et al. (2020a)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/cef2fd6c68bf5bbeb5d84b1cadb9c978/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`heilmeier2020vse`] is the closest prior work to `05`. Two neural networks decide whether to pit and which compound to fit, trained on 2014–2019 F1 timing data. Like our classifier, it imitates what teams did rather than optimising.
- **Deep sequence models.** [Sasikumar et al. (2025)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/4da2023f2da15b69bfe0f75110c92f59/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`sasikumar2025`] predict pit timing from FastF1 data with five deep architectures and SMOTE, the best being a Bi-LSTM at F1 = 0.81. [Deep-Racing (2023)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/41b9115690a15163b5fd65f1d8409d7f/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`deepracing2023`] uses embedding layers on 2015–2022 data. Neither paper reports a forward-in-time, race-level split or a leakage audit as strict as ours, so their headline scores aren't directly comparable. Worth saying carefully in the thesis.

### 2.4 Reinforcement learning

[Thomas et al. (2026)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/aba7afc594ba5f0ba362495f27c04a71/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`thomas2026`] train an RL agent (RSRL) in a race simulator that beats hard-coded and Monte Carlo baselines, with explanations to build strategists' trust. [Fieni et al. (2025;](https://consensus.app/papers/details/af557156731e53159c1e4832e0541b99/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [2026)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d790de035fc0512d87c1d86c07e2f551/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`fieni2025`, `fieni2026`] report about 5s of RL suboptimality over a 1.5h race, then extend to several agents using only information available during a race. [Boettinger and Klotz (2023)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/7be0f5fa245b51428b45d32d182aae3f/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`boettinger2023`] apply RL to GT racing. All of these depend on a simulator. Our approach doesn't, which is a limitation (no interaction) but also a strength (every number comes from real data).

### 2.5 Tyre degradation

- [Cappello et al. (2025)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/da862bd93e1a51d68eda25a954f66c54/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`cappello2025`] model tyre degradation as a Bayesian state-space process on FastF1 data, with pit stops as state resets, evaluated by **rolling-origin cross-validation** on 19 races of 2025. It is the most direct alternative to our LightGBM tyre model: probabilistic and interpretable, where ours is a pooled point estimate.
- [Todd et al. (2025)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/7067942e118e5bb9b1f402a3f51c4d98/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`todd2025`] forecast tyre energy from Mercedes telemetry and find XGBoost competitive with deep models, which supports a tree model here too.

### 2.6 Do teams decide optimally? (sports decision analytics)

[Romer (2006)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b5fb88cf60a1501ab69a78f4c60e32c7/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`romer2006`] used dynamic programming on NFL data to show that teams' fourth-down choices depart systematically and significantly from win-maximising ones. [Goff et al. (2018)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ad1dc49310855a3ca97f884a6b588173/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`goff2018`] confirm the gap on 12 seasons and attribute much of it to risk aversion. [Brill et al. (2023)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e05fede900ad5ea8aa1d7f3a691fd454/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`brill2023`] bootstrap the models behind such "optimal" calls and find far more uncertainty than analysts usually admit. This literature supports a research framing proposed in section 5.

## 4. Methodological risks a reviewer will raise

**a) Circular evaluation (the optimizer's curse).** Real, classifier and hindsight-optimal strategies are all scored by the same pace model, and the optimum is chosen by maximising that model. [Smith and Winkler (2006)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/bfffdcbd36c45ee1b9b2dbf025b8cafc/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`smith2006`] show that choosing the best option under estimated values makes it look better than it really is, even when the estimates are unbiased. Optimizers also exploit model artefacts ([Tsiourvas 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/92f17bb19070558bac2d1e56aac119ac/?utm_source=claude_desktop) [`tsiourvas2024`]); the 6-stop and 47-stop "optima" recorded in `02`/`04` were exactly this. **Mitigation:** check the pace model's predicted total race times against drivers' actual race times; report gaps to the optimum with bootstrap intervals ([Brill 2023](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e05fede900ad5ea8aa1d7f3a691fd454/?utm_source=claude_desktop)); and frame the hindsight DP as an upper bound, not a reachable target.

*Status (2026-10-15): largely addressed.* `13_strategy_cost_validation.py` uses teammates as a natural experiment (same car and race, often different strategies). It regresses the real time difference between teammates on the model's predicted difference over their common laps, after removing each pair's skill gap measured on laps where both ran the same tyres. Slope **1.00 (95% CI 0.79–1.19)** for strategies without a stop under SC/VSC: the model prices strategy differences at the right size rather than overstating them. Strategies with a stop under SC/VSC were priced poorly at first (slope ~0.44). The cause is structural: a safety car bunches the field. Heilmeier et al.'s simulation models this with a "ghost" safety car that resets every car to the SC's time plus 0.8s per position (read in their open-source code, [TUMFTM/race-simulation](https://github.com/TUMFTM/race-simulation)). A per-car model can't track positions, so it now uses the statistical equivalent, with each piece measured from our data:
- **Typed stop prices:** SC and VSC stops cost 0.21 and 0.68 of a green stop, the median over 857 individual stops. The VSC value is close to Pitwall's independently calibrated 0.65 ([Santillana 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06495)).
- **Gap compression:** when a full SC comes out, each car's deficit to the field median shrinks to 0.21×, stable across seasons (646 car observations in 57 races). A first estimate of 0.54 turned out to be inflated by a data quirk: from mid-2026 race control writes "VSC DEPLOYED" instead of "VIRTUAL SAFETY CAR DEPLOYED", so those VSCs, which don't bunch the field, had been counted as full safety cars.
- **One cheap stop per neutralisation period:** without it, the hindsight DP stopped up to 4 times under a single Monaco safety car.

Per-race SC/VSC estimates were tried first but are too noisy (−12s to +57s) and let the DP stop up to 15 times. With all three pieces, the slope for pairs with an SC/VSC stop rose from 0.44 to 0.81, and the overall slope is 0.93 (CI 0.72–1.15). The rest is track position, which only a position-based simulation captures (future work). `06` also reports a robustness check on races with no SC/VSC, where the conclusions hold, and bootstrap 95% intervals over races for every comparison.

**b) Classifier calibration.** The classifier uses `class_weight='balanced'`, so its probabilities average about 0.26 against a real pit rate of about 3.5%, and around 40% of drivers score above 0.5 on lap 1. Several studies find that rebalancing for rare events **does not improve ranking ability but badly distorts probabilities**, and that moving the decision threshold gives the same benefit without the distortion ([Van Den Goorbergh 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/4f96f2de2573507f9ec053d4fddd5076/?utm_source=claude_desktop), [Carriero 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ac37b83163aa5d8ca8b53f8a58d05696/?utm_source=claude_desktop), [Piccininni 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/145c928d87ac5481b38fc24cb8e529f9/?utm_source=claude_desktop), [Andersen 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0d70fbde06fb533b962a4f2204c4f51c/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). The live system uses the probability directly (threshold 0.5, peak detection), so this matters. **Recommendation:** train unweighted, pick the threshold on training races, and report precision-recall AUC plus a calibration measure (Brier score or reliability curve). Or, if keeping the weighting, recalibrate afterwards with Platt or isotonic scaling ([Liu 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/13f2cf4e029c52d095cc26f3dccb9bfe/?utm_source=claude_desktop)).

*Status (2026-10-15): fixed.* The classifier is now trained without rebalancing. On the held-out races, calibration error is 0.009 vs. 0.230 for the balanced model, and the Brier score 0.032 vs. 0.131; ranking quality is about the same (average precision 0.210 vs. 0.227 on one test window, and balanced + isotonic recalibration lands at 0.209). This matches the literature. A second problem turned up while fixing it: the decision threshold had been picked on the **test races** themselves, inflating the reported F1. It's now chosen on the last training season and applied to the test races unchanged (honest F1 0.278 vs. the previous test-tuned 0.288), and the same threshold is used live instead of a fixed 0.5.

**c) One test window.** All results come from a single block of 21 races, 15 after filtering. **Recommendation:** add rolling-origin evaluation, for example one test block per season from 2023, each trained on everything before it. This gives several estimates and a spread ([Cerqueira 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/2d7493f839e35467a2e51f7d216cc8c2/?utm_source=claude_desktop), [Tashman 2000](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ca968323bf685b608ae0a3eee387ebb1/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). The as-of machinery in `12` already does most of the work.

*Status (2026-10-15): done.* `05` predicts every season 2022–2026 with a model trained only on earlier races. Per season: average precision 0.17–0.23 and calibration error ≤0.016. `06` replays all 78 dry races from those predictions (1252 driver-races) next to the pinned 15-race test set. With the safety-car modelling in place (4a), the multi-stop ranking rule's advantage over real teams (paired median 4.2s, 95% CI 3.1–5.5s) holds across seasons and in the 24 races without any SC/VSC (4.0s, CI 1.3–7.2s). On the 15 pinned races alone it's 5.5s (CI 3.0–7.8s); before safety cars were modelled it wasn't significant there, which shows why one window wasn't enough. The threshold rule, the only one usable live, is 1.0s *worse* than the teams (CI 0.0–2.2s), and 3.4s worse without SC/VSC.

**d) One car, no interaction.** No traffic, track position, undercut or rival reactions are modelled. [Aguad 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/79d6f7e3b10b5b3a8d18fa6208575976/?utm_source=claude_desktop) shows interaction is worth more than 15% in winning odds. This should be stated as a scope limit. It also explains why real teams pit under a safety car even when our model calls it "too close to call": they are protecting track position.

**e) The hindsight optimum can't be reached.** It knows future safety cars. Comparisons against it overstate how far real teams fall short. An **ex-ante** benchmark, the best decision given what was known at the time, would be fairer, and the live stochastic DP already computes one (section 5).

**f) The cost model wants to stop much later than teams.** *Found 2026-10-15, open.* Live replays put the classifier's pit calls on the right laps (Spain 2024: 36 of 42 stops called for that exact lap, a lap ahead). But at those laps the expected-cost model mostly says *stay out*. For green-flag stops its first "pit now" comes a median 10 laps after the team's real stop in Spain 2024 and 6 laps after in Canada 2023, and for 17 of Spain's 42 stops not within 15 laps. The teammate validation (4a) doesn't catch this, because teammates usually stop within a few laps of each other, so it tests small timing differences rather than 10-lap ones. Two likely causes, not yet separated:
- **Undercut and track position:** teams stop early to jump rivals on fresh tyres, which a per-car time model gives no value. That matches the interaction effects in [Aguad 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/79d6f7e3b10b5b3a8d18fa6208575976/?utm_source=claude_desktop).
- **Censored tyre data:** teams change tyres before they fall away, so the tyre model rarely observes late-stint degradation and probably extrapolates it too optimistically, making long stints look cheap.

*Update (Phase 1, same day):* the safety-car option value was ruled out: the delay is identical without it. The cause is "too few stops". In Spain 2024 every team two-stopped, but the model's optimum was a one-stop for all 20 drivers, and across all races it chose fewer stops than the team in 34% of driver-races. Within stints, observed wear *slows* with age for every compound, so race data are censored and also self-selected. Correcting that from the data failed the teammate validation twice (slopes 0.18 and 0.47): real stints that ran long were genuinely fine, so a forced drop-off misprices them. Conclusion: race data can confirm the tyre model where teams have run a strategy, but can't show that an untried one would fail. The tyre model keeps a monotone constraint (validated, slope 0.91) and a safety-net drop-off beyond the longest stint ever run on a compound (0.87). Whether e.g. a one-stop is realistic at a circuit is left to a pre-race default strategy built from Pirelli's prediction and earlier races there, which encodes the teams' revealed knowledge (Phase 2).

*Update (Phase 2, 2026-10-19):* the default strategy and a live policy built on it.
- **Default plan (`14`).** Pirelli's published pre-race prediction where we have one (`reference/pirelli_strategies.csv`, 18 of the 19 dry test races, collected by hand with sources; Pirelli's first-listed option, never chosen by outcome), otherwise what finishers did at the circuit before (as of the race, recency-weighted). Over the 12 test races with both, Pirelli and history are level (median −0.3 s per driver for Pirelli, 95% CI −3.1 to +5.4). Pirelli helps most where history misleads: Australia 2026, the first race of the new rules, by 24 s. It's worst where its first-listed option wasn't what teams ran (Hungary 2026, Mexico 2025).
- **Live policy (`14`'s `PlanFollower`, replayed in `06`, live in `09`).** Follow the plan and leave it only when the expected-cost DP's margin beats its own error (1 s): pit under an SC/VSC if clearly cheaper, and move a green-flag stop only inside the plan's window. A stop that comes early or late re-spaces the rest of the plan to keep its stint proportions. Keeping green-flag changes inside the window is what stops the cost model's lean towards late stops (above) from taking over.
- **Result** (after the circuit-key rebuild). Over the 78 rolling-origin races, the policy loses a median 4.3 s to the hindsight optimum against the teams' 11.2 s: a paired 4.4 s better than the teams (95% CI 2.7–6.2 s), better in 72% of driver-races. In the 24 green-flag races it's 2.5 s better (CI 0.1–5.1 s). It's the first rule here that is both usable live and better than the teams; the earlier live rule (classifier threshold) was 2.1 s worse. Most of the gain is the SC/VSC reaction: plan only −0.9 s, plan + SC/VSC −4.3 s. Scored in finishing position (item 7 in section 6), the gain holds overall but not in green-flag races.
- **Caveat.** The policy is scored by the same cost model it decides with. The teammate validation (4a) supports that model's pricing, including SC/VSC stops, but not the absolute size of the gain.

This also bears on the backtest's "real teams lose ~11s to the hindsight optimum": part of that gap may be the model's late-stopping bias rather than team error. **Next step:** test the tyre model on the longest real stints (predicted vs. observed pace by tyre age, beyond the usual stint length), and compare stop timing against rivals' undercuts.

## 5. A possibly stronger research question

The system now has the pieces to ask a cleaner question than "can a classifier beat teams?":

> **Do F1 teams make expected-time-optimal pit decisions, given only what they knew at the time?**

This mirrors [Romer (2006)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b5fb88cf60a1501ab69a78f4c60e32c7/?utm_source=claude_desktop) for F1:
1. **Score every decision ex ante.** For every lap of every dry race, use the as-of models (`12`) and the expected-cost stochastic DP (`10`) to compute the cost of pitting versus staying out, using only information available at the time. That gives an ex-ante optimal decision per lap, with no hindsight.
2. **Compare with what teams did.** Measure how often teams depart from the ex-ante optimum, by how much, and where (safety-car laps, first or second stop, team, era).
3. **Quantify uncertainty** by bootstrapping races, as [Brill (2023)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e05fede900ad5ea8aa1d7f3a691fd454/?utm_source=claude_desktop) does, so that "too close to call" becomes a formal result rather than a display label.
4. **Explain the departures.** The trigger classifier changes role: from a would-be decision-maker to a model of team behaviour. Its features (rival gaps, track position, rival tyre age, recent pit activity) can test whether departures are explained by things the time-only model ignores, as risk aversion did in [Goff (2018)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ad1dc49310855a3ca97f884a6b588173/?utm_source=claude_desktop).

**Why it's stronger:**
- **It avoids risk 4e.** The benchmark is ex ante, not hindsight.
- **It turns risk 4d into a finding.** "Teams deviate from time-optimal decisions in ways explained by track position" is a result, not a limitation.
- **It has a falsifiable answer, with precedent** in the economics and sports analytics literature.
- **It reuses nearly everything already built.**

**Evidence so far (2026-10-29, section 6 item 7):** a live-usable policy that re-plans the whole race by expected position and points finishes about 0.8–0.95 places better than the teams' real strategies per driver-race, almost entirely by making *fewer* stops than they did. Where this can be checked against teams that really ran fewer stops, the models price it right, and the extra spread it brings is upside, so risk aversion doesn't explain the teams' extra stops either (it would take λ ≈ 2–3 in mean − λ·SD). That is a concrete, falsifiable version of this section's question, with the censoring and self-selection caveats in item 7.

The current framing remains viable. This is an option to discuss with the supervisor.
